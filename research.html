Research

The lab develops predictive multiphysics frameworks for how fluids, stresses, temperature and reactions co-evolve in porous and fractured rock, and validates them against fiber-optic measurements from the field.

What is distributed fiber-optic sensing?

A fiber-optic cable, the same kind that carries internet traffic, is installed along a well or a mine tunnel. An instrument at the surface fires laser pulses into it and analyzes the tiny amount of light scattered back.

Because the light changes wherever the cable is stretched, squeezed, shaken or warmed, a single cable becomes thousands of sensors spaced a few feet apart, recording continuously. Depending on what is measured, this is called distributed acoustic sensing (DAS), distributed strain sensing (DSS) or distributed temperature sensing (DTS).

Fiber-optic sensing and fracture characterization

  • DAS, low-frequency DAS
  • Distributed strain sensing
  • Distributed temperature sensing

When a hydraulic fracture grows toward a neighboring well, a fiber in that well records a characteristic pattern of stretching and squeezing well before the fracture arrives. These signals are used to measure fracture orientation, propagation speed and geometry, and strain data are inverted to characterize fractures quantitatively.

Field work includes a decade of data from the Denver-Julesburg Basin and cross-well strain monitoring of multi-crew zipper fracturing. Current work extends fiber monitoring to carbon storage reservoirs and to deformation and seepage in critical-mineral mines.

Coupled multiphysics modeling

  • Thermal-hydrological-mechanical-reactive (THM-R)
  • MOOSE / PorousFlow
  • Compositional reservoir simulation

Fiber data are only as useful as the physics used to interpret them. Coupled fluid-flow and geomechanics models predict how a reservoir responds to injection and what a fiber should record; these predictions are then compared with field measurements.

Applications include large-scale CO2 storage in Southern Colorado, repurposing a depleted natural CO2 dome for permanent storage, wellbore integrity and leakage risk, CO2 enhanced oil recovery, and underground hydrogen storage. A growing direction is chemo-mechanical coupling: how mineral reactions change porosity and permeability over time.

Physics-informed machine learning

  • Time-series forecasting
  • Data mining of small datasets
  • Discrete fracture network modeling

Machine learning helps where full simulations are too slow or field data are too sparse. Past work compared ARIMA, LSTM and Prophet models for production forecasting, identified key production drivers from small datasets, and combined machine learning with flow modeling to optimize production well geometry for an enhanced geothermal system.

At Clemson, the lab is integrating AI-based analysis with coupled simulations to interpret fiber-optic signals faster and with quantified uncertainty.

From industry to academia

Before joining academia, Dr. Ning worked as a reservoir engineer at Crowheart Energy in Denver, building production type curves, optimizing well planning and fracture stage spacing, and automating data analysis in Python, Power BI and Spotfire. That experience shapes how the lab chooses problems: methods should be useful to the people making decisions in the field.
